\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Answer to the research question} The answer depends on which output is used, because the surrogate does not return a mutually compatible set of displacements and forces as an FE analysis does. Three uses should be distinguished.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Member-force screening.} The directly predicted, equilibrium-projected member forces are the most reliable output. Within the training design space and on the design-informed frames the median error of the end-moment field is 2.4\% (ID) and 2.0\% (DES), the largest end moment, largest axial force and reactions are within 1\% at the median, and the forces are exactly in equilibrium with the loads. For this use the two-stage referral rule applies: with thresholds fixed in advance it accepts 99\% of the ID frames with 1.4\% of the accepted frames above a 10\% end-moment error, and it refers most frames of the shifted sets while keeping that rate at 1--2\%. Among the accepted frames, the other force quantities exceed a 10\% error in at most 0.7\% of the frames, but this is an empirical observation, not a guarantee.
\item \emph{Kinematic screening.} The predicted displacements, rotations and drifts are validated separately (Table~\ref{tab:extended}) and are less accurate (median 4.5\% for the horizontal displacement field, 95th percentile of the largest-drift error 20\% on ID). The referral rule does not protect them: up to 38\% of the accepted frames have a drift error above 10\%. They are suitable for ranking and trend studies, not for checking a drift limit near its threshold.
\item \emph{Complete structural analysis.} This use is not established. The projected forces are not compatible with the predicted displacements (Section~\ref{sec:compatresults}), so the two outputs cannot be combined as if they came from one FE solution, for example to check a member against both its forces and its end rotations.
\end{enumerate}
The surrogate can therefore reduce the number of repeated FE analyses in member-force and kinematic screening tasks inside a validated domain, with FE analysis retained for any final structural verification and for the frames that the rule refers. Outside the training space the median errors roughly triple and the 95th percentile of the moment error exceeds 20\%; the surrogate is then usable only together with the referral rule.

\paragraph{What the constraints buy} The exact constraints contribute in different ways. Dimensional scaling and load-case separation are an \emph{accuracy} device: they remove the need to learn the dependence on the load intensities and on $E$ and make the lateral and gravity responses equally well conditioned. Used together, they halve the displacement errors of a conventional GNN with the same architecture (Table~\ref{tab:featabl}); the statics-derived features add a smaller benefit, mainly for the displacements under unseen load patterns. The improvement should therefore be credited to the combination of dimensionless scaling, load-case separation and statics features, and mostly to the first two, rather than to the statics features alone. The equilibrium projection is primarily an \emph{admissibility} device. It guarantees static equilibrium, which a soft penalty does not achieve; with a variance-weighted metric it also reduces the axial-force and reaction errors by factors of 2--5, but its effect on the moment errors is small (0.1 to 0.5 percentage points on average over five runs) and statistically clear only on some test sets. With a poorly chosen metric the projection degrades the moments, so the metric is an essential part of the method. The projection does not enforce compatibility: it slightly increases the mismatch between the predicted forces and the forces implied by the predicted displacements, and that mismatch, dominated by the axial terms, is large. Recovering forces from predicted displacements gives compatibility but loses accuracy and equilibrium, by a wide margin. Imposing both conditions exactly amounts to solving the FE problem; using the predicted displacements as a warm start of an iterative solver did not help either.

\paragraph{Architecture} A StructGNN-style network with storey pseudo nodes, given the same inputs and targets, reached similar accuracy, better for displacements on unseen geometries and slightly worse for moments, at seven times the training cost; global pooling and deeper message passing gave the proposed network the same displacement gains. The architecture of the learned model is therefore not the main contribution of this work. The exact constraints, the uncertainty estimate and the decision rules built on them are model-agnostic and could be wrapped around any of these networks.

\paragraph{Uncertainty as a decision tool} The two uses of uncertainty have distinct indicators. At deployment, the ensemble spread is a good ranking of the errors for in-distribution, geometrically extrapolated and design-informed frames, but under a change of load pattern the ensemble is confidently wrong (coverage of the $\pm2\sigma$ intervals 67\% instead of 92\%). A domain check on simple load-pattern descriptors, which are known before any analysis, detects most of those frames, and a split-conformal bound calibrated on validation data turns the spread into a threshold with a stated coverage. During training-set construction, acquisition by ensemble variance was worse than random sampling, whereas acquisition by the equilibrium residual of the raw prediction, which requires no labels, was the best rule in all five repetitions and reached the accuracy of random sampling on unseen geometries with half the FE analyses. Diversity-aware batch selection did not improve the median errors further but reduced the variation between repetitions.

\paragraph{Limitations} The study is restricted to planar, regular, multi-storey moment frames with prismatic members, rigid joints, uniformly distributed beam loads and floor-level lateral forces, analysed with first-order linear elasticity and a uniform Young's modulus. The design-informed set uses simplified sizing rules, not code-compliant designs, and it shares the topology family of the training data. The graph baselines are re-implementations from the published descriptions with adaptations stated in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}; in particular, the poor performance of the stiffness-residual loss in our Leng-style implementation may reflect our scaling of the residual rather than the original method. The adaptive study is retrospective: candidates were labelled in advance and their labels revealed only on request, which gives exact budget accounting but not the scheduling overheads of a live FE loop. The conformal guarantee assumes exchangeability with the calibration frames and holds only approximately for the in-domain part of shifted distributions. Timings were measured on one CPU with Python implementations of the surrogate and of all three solvers.

\paragraph{Outlook} Natural extensions are a mixed formulation that also enforces compatibility through the member flexibilities, three-dimensional frames, braced systems and second-order (P-$\Delta$) analysis, for which the FE cost is higher and superposition no longer holds exactly, and conformal methods that adapt to covariate shift.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Equilibrium-projected graph neural network surrogates for the first-order linear analysis of planar moment frames were developed and evaluated on 9{,}500 PyNite analyses, verified against an independent stiffness solver, OpenSeesPy and closed-form solutions. A graph network ensemble predicts nodal displacements and member basic forces in a dimensionless, load-case-separated form, so that superposition, homogeneity in the loads, the $1/E$ scaling of displacements and the boundary conditions hold exactly; an equilibrium projection with a variance-weighted metric makes the predicted forces statically admissible; a domain check and a conformal bound on the ensemble spread decide which frames are referred to PyNite at deployment; and the equilibrium residual of the raw prediction selects new FE analyses during training. The main conclusions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item On in-distribution and design-informed frames the surrogate reaches median end-moment errors of 2.4\% and 2.0\% (five independent runs: $2.37\pm0.03\%$ and $1.99\pm0.08\%$), axial-force errors of 0.6\% and 0.3\%, and horizontal displacement errors of 4.5\% and 4.4\%, four to nine times lower than the best of five tabular baselines given identical features.
\item On unseen geometries and unseen load patterns the median moment errors are 7.5\% and 8.7\% (best tabular baseline 22.1\% and 16.8\%), but the 95th percentiles exceed 20\%.
\item The combined use of dimensionless scaling and load-case separation halves the displacement errors of a conventional graph network; statics-derived features add a smaller, mainly displacement-related benefit. A StructGNN-style network performs similarly to the proposed network, so the benefits of the framework lie in its exact constraints and decision rules rather than in the network architecture. Displacement-only networks with force recovery give much less accurate forces.
\item The equilibrium projection reduces the out-of-balance force from 4--16\% (raw ensemble) and up to 120\% (baselines) to below $10^{-12}$ for every model, reduces the axial-force errors by factors of 2--5 and lowers the median moment errors by 0.1--0.5 percentage points on average over five runs with a variance-weighted metric, whereas a Euclidean metric increases them. It slightly increases the displacement--force incompatibility.
\item A two-stage referral rule, with the domain threshold and the conformal quantile calibrated on two separate sets of new frames, covers the end-moment error of in-distribution frames as guaranteed (90.7\% for a nominal 90\%), accepts 99\% of them with 1.4\% of the accepted frames above a 10\% end-moment error, and refers 71--73\% of the unseen frames while keeping that rate at 1.1--1.7\%. The rule is calibrated for the end-moment field only: the other member forces of accepted frames were also accurate, but the drift, rotation and displacement errors of up to 38\% of the accepted frames exceeded 10\%.
\item Acquiring training frames by the equilibrium residual of the raw prediction reached the accuracy of 1{,}200 random analyses on unseen geometries with 600 analyses in five repetitions, whereas acquisition by ensemble variance was worse than random sampling.
\item \input{cost_conclusion}
\end{enumerate}
The framework is best viewed as an equilibrium-enforced, uncertainty-aware surrogate for selected structural-response screening tasks, in particular member-force screening, that knows the limits of its training data. It is not a replacement for a complete FE analysis, whose displacements and forces are mutually compatible, and FE analysis remains necessary for final structural verification.
