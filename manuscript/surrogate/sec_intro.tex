\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Many tasks in structural engineering require the same structural system to be analysed many times: preliminary sizing and design-space exploration, code-based checks over large numbers of load combinations and variants, portfolio-scale assessment of existing buildings, optimisation and reliability analysis. Each analysis is cheap in isolation, but the number of repetitions makes finite element (FE) analysis the dominant cost of these loops. Surrogate models, which replace the FE solver by a fast approximation learned from a limited number of FE runs, have therefore a long history in engineering design~\cite{forrester2008surrogate} and reliability analysis~\cite{bichon2008egra,echard2011akmcs}, and machine learning (ML) has substantially widened the range of quantities that can be emulated~\cite{xie2020eqml}.

For structural assessment, however, a surrogate is only useful if its output can be trusted in the way an FE result is trusted. Three obstacles stand in the way. First, purely data-driven models carry no guarantee that their outputs are mechanically admissible: predicted member forces need not be in equilibrium with the applied loads, the predicted response need not scale linearly with the loads in a linear analysis, and boundary conditions are only approximately respected. An engineer who checks the reactions against the loads will immediately notice such defects. Second, accuracy reported on test data drawn from the training distribution says little about frames whose geometry or loading differ from those seen in training, which is the normal situation in practice. Third, the cost of generating training data is often treated as fixed, although the FE budget is usually the scarcest resource, and the surrogate itself does not indicate when its prediction should not be used.

Physics-informed machine learning~\cite{karniadakis2021piml} addresses the first obstacle by embedding physical knowledge in the learning model. Physics-informed neural networks penalise the residual of the governing equations in the loss~\cite{raissi2019pinn} and have been applied to solid mechanics~\cite{haghighat2021solid}; in structural dynamics, physics-guided convolutional and recurrent networks have been used to model the seismic response of buildings~\cite{zhang2020phycnn,zhang2020phylstm}. A penalty term, however, only encourages the constraint and leaves a residual violation whose size depends on the training. Constraints can instead be imposed exactly, either through the network output layer~\cite{beucler2021constraints} or through hard-constraint formulations~\cite{lu2021hard}. Graph neural networks (GNNs)~\cite{battaglia2018relational,gilmer2017mpnn} are a natural representation for structures whose topology varies: they have been used to simulate particle and mesh-based physics~\cite{sanchez2020learning,pfaff2021meshgraphnets}, to predict stress and deformation fields~\cite{maurizi2022gnn} and to build reusable surrogates for truss structures~\cite{whalen2022truss}. On the side of uncertainty, deep ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} provide a simple estimate of epistemic uncertainty, although its quality degrades under distribution shift~\cite{ovadia2019shift}; and adaptive sampling driven by surrogate uncertainty is well established for Kriging models in structural reliability~\cite{bichon2008egra,echard2011akmcs} and more broadly as active learning~\cite{settles2009active}.

\subsection{Recent developments in mechanics-informed graph learning for structural analysis}\label{sec:review}

Graph representations have become the default choice for learning the static response of skeletal structures, because joints and members map directly onto nodes and edges and the same network can be applied to structures of different size. Table~\ref{tab:review} compares the studies closest to the present one; entries marked ``n.r.'' were not reported in the text we could access, and the comparison is limited to what each paper states.

Whalen and Mueller~\cite{whalen2022truss} trained a graph surrogate on linear FE results of parametric trusses to predict nodal displacements from geometry, supports and loads. Their model is purely data-driven (mean-squared-error loss), and they reported that it did not generalise well to topologies excluded from training, which motivated transfer learning. Maurizi et al.~\cite{maurizi2022gnn} predicted stress, strain and deformation fields of materials and structures with a message-passing network, again without mechanics constraints. Parisi et al.~\cite{parisi2024mism} proposed a mechanics-informed surrogate model (MISM) in which the structural topology enters through the graph and the section properties through edge features of an edge-conditioned convolution. It predicts the nodal displacements and rotations of two- and three-dimensional frame and truss structures, but uses a plain mean-absolute-error loss with no equilibrium or compatibility term, and was tested on a random split of the same generator. Chou et al.~\cite{chou2024structgnn} introduced StructGNN for the static analysis of multi-storey buildings. Its graph adds one pseudo node per storey, connected to all joints of that floor as a proxy for a rigid diaphragm, and uses as many message-passing layers as storeys plus one so that information can travel the full building height. Trained on about 2{,}000 buildings of two to six storeys, it predicted displacements and member forces with high accuracy and kept useful accuracy on taller seven- to nine-storey buildings. It does not enforce equilibrium and gives no uncertainty estimate. Kuo et al.~\cite{kuo2025gnn} used message-passing networks to return displacements, shear forces and bending moments of multi-storey frames under wind for interactive design and reported promising generalisation to taller configurations. Most recently, Leng et al.~\cite{leng2026mcgnn} proposed a mechanically constrained GNN for planar frames with member loads. Their network predicts only the nodal displacements, adds the free-DOF stiffness residual and global equilibrium residuals to the loss as soft penalties, and recovers member forces from the displacements through the element stiffness relations with a fixed-end correction for member loads. Displacement-based recovery makes the forces compatible with the predicted displacements, but equilibrium then holds only to the extent that the penalty is minimised.

Three gaps remain. First, no study in Table~\ref{tab:review} enforces nodal equilibrium exactly; where it appears at all, it is a soft penalty. Second, none quantifies predictive uncertainty or uses it to decide when to fall back on the FE solver or where to spend new FE analyses. Third, out-of-distribution testing, where present, has concerned taller buildings only; loading patterns outside the training distribution have not been examined. The present study addresses these gaps for linear planar frames and compares directly against re-implementations of the StructGNN-style and Leng-style designs (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{Objectives and contributions}

This paper develops and evaluates, for planar moment-resisting frames under first-order linear static analysis, a workflow in which (a) the predicted member forces satisfy nodal static equilibrium exactly rather than on average, (b) the known linear structure of the problem (superposition, homogeneity in the loads, scaling with Young's modulus, boundary conditions) is imposed rather than learned, (c) predictive uncertainty is used at deployment to refer doubtful predictions back to the FE solver and an equilibrium residual is used during training to choose new FE analyses, and (d) the result is tested against conventional regression, machine-learning and graph-learning baselines on geometries and load patterns outside the training distribution. The open-source PyNite FE library~\cite{pynite} generates all data; an independent stiffness solver and OpenSeesPy~\cite{zhu2018openseespy} verify it. The research question is whether a carefully validated surrogate can predict displacements and member forces accurately enough to reduce repeated FE analysis. Throughout, ``mechanics-constrained'' refers to exact static equilibrium and exact linear scaling; compatibility between the predicted displacements and forces is measured but not enforced (Section~\ref{sec:compat}).

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A graph-network surrogate in which load-case superposition, homogeneity in the load intensities, the $1/E$ scaling of displacements and the boundary conditions hold exactly by construction, with exact statics quantities and classical approximations supplied as inputs.
\item An equilibrium projection that maps any predicted member-force field to the nearest statically admissible field in a variance-weighted metric, with an error-reduction guarantee in that metric (Proposition~\ref{prop:projection}) and an analysis of its effect on displacement--force compatibility.
\item A two-stage deployment rule, a domain check on the frame's geometry and load-pattern descriptors followed by a split-conformal threshold on the ensemble spread, both calibrated on data separate from the test sets, and a residual-guided adaptive FE sampling scheme evaluated over five repetitions.
\item A benchmark of 7{,}500 PyNite analyses, verified against two independent solvers and closed-form solutions, with in-distribution, unseen-geometry, unseen-load-pattern and design-driven test sets; comparisons with five tabular baselines and five graph-learning baselines, including StructGNN-style and Leng-style re-implementations; and statistics over five independent training runs. Code, data generator and trained weights are released.
\end{enumerate}
