Timings were measured serially on an otherwise idle 4-core CPU (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). A PyNite analysis of one frame (two load cases, including model construction and result extraction) costs 37.5\,ms for ID frames and 104.7\,ms for the larger OOD-G frames. Evaluated in batches, the proposed surrogate costs 2.7\,ms (ID), 7.0\,ms (OOD-G) and 3.7\,ms (OOD-L) per frame, including the Python feature construction (the largest part), the five-network ensemble and the projection, i.e.\ 10--15 times less than PyNite (Table~\ref{tab:timing}); the projection itself takes 0.1--0.6\,ms. For a single frame evaluated in isolation, the framework overhead of the networks dominates and the surrogate (17--82\,ms) is not faster than PyNite. With 20\% of the frames referred back to PyNite, the expected speed-up of the batched workflow drops to 3.3--3.8 (Table~\ref{tab:uq}).

These savings must be set against the one-off cost of building the surrogate: training one network on the 3{,}000 training frames took 487\,s, so the five-network ensemble costs about 41\,min of CPU time, while the 3{,}000 PyNite analyses of the training set cost only about 2\,min. With 20\% referral, the surrogate therefore pays for itself only after roughly $9\times10^4$ ID frames (about $3\times10^4$ of the larger OOD-G frames). For first-order linear analysis of frames of this size, PyNite is already fast, and the surrogate is worthwhile only for very large numbers of repeated evaluations, for batched evaluation inside optimisation or uncertainty-propagation loops, or when the training cost is shared across many studies. The balance shifts in favour of the surrogate as the cost of a single FE analysis grows, but this paper does not test surrogates for the more expensive analyses (nonlinear, dynamic, or much larger models) where that would be the case.
