\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Minimum-weight design of steel moment frames with discrete catalogue sections is a mature
topic in structural optimisation. Genetic algorithms \citep{Camp1998,Pezeshk2000}, harmony
search \citep{Degertekin2008} and a large family of other metaheuristics \citep{SakaGeem2013}
have been used to select hot-rolled sections that satisfy code strength and serviceability
checks at least weight. Almost all of this work is \emph{deterministic}: the optimiser drives
the governing load-and-resistance-factor design (LRFD) checks to unity and treats the partial
factors of the code as a sufficient representation of uncertainty. The partial factors,
however, were calibrated for members and typical load ratios \citep{Ellingwood1980,Galambos1982};
they do not guarantee a uniform reliability for an optimised \emph{system}, whose members are
all deliberately pushed to their limits and whose governing constraints may be strength in one
member group and drift in another.

Reliability-based design optimisation (RBDO) replaces or supplements the deterministic checks by
explicit constraints on failure probabilities \citep{Frangopol1985,Enevoldsen1994,Valdebenito2010,Aoues2010}.
Table~\ref{tab:literature} summarises the studies closest to the present one. Reliability-based
optimisation of steel frames with discrete sections and Monte Carlo simulation was pioneered by
Papadrakakis, Lagaros and co-workers \citep{PapadrakakisLagaros2002,Papadrakakis2004,LagarosPapadrakakis2007}.
\citet{Papadrakakis2004} optimised a seismically loaded 3-storey planar moment frame with evolution
strategies. They imposed the Eurocode 3 and 8 checks in both the deterministic and the
reliability-based problem, and added a Monte Carlo constraint on the probability that the interstorey
drift exceeds 4\% of the storey height. The reliability-based optimum was heavier than the
deterministic one (their text gives approximately 26\%; the volumes in their Table~6 differ by 34\%).
The deterministic optimum had a failure probability of $9.3\times10^{-2}$. Their failure probabilities
were estimated from 5000 (MCS) or 1000 (LHS) samples, without confidence intervals.
\citet{TruongKim2017} embedded plastic-hinge advanced analysis in the RBDO of steel space frames under
gravity and wind with harmony search. Their RBDO also kept the deterministic strength and $H/400$ drift
constraints, and used a sampling estimator verified against crude Monte Carlo on benchmark problems.
Drift was not a probabilistic constraint, and the failure probabilities of the final frame designs were
not re-estimated independently. Multi-objective genetic algorithms have been used for probabilistic
performance-based design of steel moment frames \citep{Foley2007} and for weight--reliability fronts
of transmission towers \citep{Mathakari2007}. The system reliability of frames designed by advanced
analysis has been quantified without optimisation \citep{Buonopane2006,Zhang2016}.

Imposing code checks in addition to a reliability constraint is therefore not new
\citep{Papadrakakis2004,TruongKim2017}, and neither is the finding that a reliability constraint can
increase the weight \citep{Papadrakakis2004}. In the two closest studies, which we read in full, we did
not find (i) a comparison of reliability-based optima under a code-calibrated gravity--wind model with
deterministic optima of the same frames, including deterministic designs with tightened drift limits
and RBDO with and without the code checks; (ii) a probabilistic serviceability (drift) constraint
next to the ULS constraint; (iii) confidence intervals for the failure probabilities of the final
designs, or a re-estimate of them with independent samples; (iv) a validation of the reliability
surrogate against full second-order re-analysis in the failure tail of several optimised designs; or
(v) a release of the code and raw results. For the other studies in Table~\ref{tab:literature},
which we could assess only from their abstracts and records, we make no such claim.

This paper addresses the research question: \emph{can uncertainty-aware optimisation produce
lighter steel frame designs than code-based optimisation while satisfying explicit reliability and
serviceability constraints?} Steel mass is the objective; it is used as a proxy for material cost,
and its relation to a broader cost is examined with an illustrative cost model. The contribution is
not a new optimisation algorithm, but a computational workflow, with demonstrated accuracy, that
makes probabilistic constraints practical in discrete steel-section optimisation and allows an
honest comparison with code-based design. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an exact superposition surrogate that turns five linear analyses per design (PyNite
\citep{PyNite}) and the AISC~360-16 $B_1$--$B_2$ amplification into a vectorised Monte Carlo
evaluation of a series-system ultimate limit state (ULS) and two serviceability limit states (SLS)
for $5\times10^4$--$10^6$ samples per design, combined with a sample average approximation (SAA)
\citep{Kleywegt2002} that makes the reliability constraints deterministic functions of the design;
\item four formulations solved with the same optimiser on the same frames: deterministic design
optimisation (DDO) with several drift limits, RBDO with reliability constraints only, a
\emph{code-compliant} RBDO (RBDO-C) that also enforces every LRFD and serviceability check, and a
bi-objective mass--reliability formulation;
\item a statistical treatment of the reliability estimates (exact binomial confidence intervals,
failure counts, an explicit acceptance rule, and a confidence-bounded variant of the reliability
constraint), and an assessment of optimiser robustness by repeated runs, SAA variants and
exhaustive enumeration of a reduced design space;
\item a verification chain from closed-form solutions and an independent solver (OpenSeesPy
\citep{McKenna2010,Zhu2018}) to full P-$\Delta$ re-analysis Monte Carlo of ten designs from six
frame configurations, including the failure tail, the failure classification and a model-based
treatment of the unanalysed part of the tail; and
\item an open release of the code, input data, seeds and raw results with a one-command reproduction.
\end{enumerate}
What is demonstrated is restricted to the seven regular planar steel moment frame configurations
studied (three to nine storeys under gravity and wind), with a second-order elastic, component-based
ULS and the stated probabilistic model. Generality to
three-dimensional frames, seismic actions, inelastic collapse reliability and full cost models is
\emph{not} demonstrated here.

Section~\ref{sec:framework} presents the frame family, the design checks, the probabilistic
model, the reliability estimation and the optimisation formulations. Section~\ref{sec:verification}
verifies the structural and reliability models. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the optimiser
robustness, the comparison of deterministic and reliability-based designs, the cost--reliability
trade-off and the parametric, economic and sensitivity studies. Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses the interpretation and limitations and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Framework}\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Parameterised frame family}\label{sec:frames}

The framework generates regular planar moment frames with $n_s$ storeys and $n_b$ bays of
width $b$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:frame}). All beam--column connections are rigid and column bases are
fixed. The ground storey is 4.5~m high and the upper storeys 3.8~m. Frames are spaced at
7.5~m, which defines the tributary width of floor loads and wind pressure. Nominal floor loads
are $q_D = 4.0$~kPa (slab, finishes and services) and $q_L = 2.4$~kPa (office occupancy); the
roof carries 3.0~kPa dead and 1.0~kPa live load. Member self-weight is added to the dead load.
The nominal 50-year wind is represented by a net (windward plus leeward) pressure $p_w$,
converted to storey forces from the tributary storey heights and distributed equally to the
floor nodes, which mimics a rigid floor diaphragm.

Members are grouped by storey groups of two storeys (three for the nine-storey frame); every
group has three discrete design variables: the exterior column, the interior column and the
beam section. Columns are selected from 20 W14 shapes (W14$\times$48 to W14$\times$311) and
beams from 28 W16--W30 shapes. All sections are ASTM A992 ($F_y = 345$~MPa,
$E = 200$~GPa). Section properties were taken from the AISC Shapes Database
\citep{AISCShapes}; as a guard against transcription errors, each entry was checked against
the fillet-free three-plate idealisation of the section, giving ratios of tabulated to plate
values of 0.99--1.03 for $A$, $I_x$, $Z_x$ and $I_y$ in all cases. The catalogues are sorted by
area so that an index step is a monotonic change in mass. A constructability constraint
requires column areas not to increase with height.

The base frame has six storeys, three 8-m bays and $p_w=1.2$~kPa. The parametric family
varies one parameter at a time (Table~\ref{tab:frames}): $n_s\in\{3,6,9\}$,
$b\in\{6,8,10\}$~m and $p_w\in\{0.8,1.2,1.6\}$~kPa. A reduced problem, the base frame with one
section group over all six storeys (three variables, $20\times20\times28 = 11\,200$ designs), is
used to check the optimiser against exhaustive enumeration.

\subsection{Structural analysis by superposition of PyNite unit cases}\label{sec:superposition}

For each candidate design $\mathbf{x}$, PyNite \citep{PyNite} solves five first-order linear
elastic load cases with nominal stiffness: dead load $D$ (including self-weight), live load
$L$, wind $W$, and two lateral cases $N_D$ and $N_L$ whose floor forces equal the dead and live
gravity load of each floor. Any realisation of the loading, with intensities
$(d,\ell,w)$ (multiples of the nominal $D$, $L$, $W$) and a global out-of-plumbness angle
$\theta$, then produces the first-order response
\begin{equation}
r = \underbrace{d\,r_D + \ell\,r_L}_{r_{nt}}
  + \underbrace{w\,r_W + \theta\,(d\,r_{N_D} + \ell\,r_{N_L})}_{r_{lt}},
\label{eq:superposition}
\end{equation}
split into the no-translation part $r_{nt}$ and the lateral-translation part $r_{lt}$
required by the approximate second-order analysis of AISC~360 Appendix~8. Because member
forces of a frame with uniformly scaled modulus $eE$ are independent of $e$ and displacements
scale with $1/e$, a global random modulus factor is also represented exactly. Responses
extracted per unit case are column end moments, axial and shear forces; beam moments at 11
stations along the span, axial and end shear forces; mid-span beam deflections relative to the
chord; and floor-average inter-storey drifts.

Second-order effects are introduced with the $B_1$--$B_2$ method. For storey $i$,
\begin{equation}
B_{2,i} = \left(1-\frac{P_{story,i}}{P_{e,story,i}}\right)^{-1},\qquad
P_{e,story,i} = R_M \,\tau\, e\,\frac{H_i h_i}{\Delta_{H,i}},
\label{eq:B2}
\end{equation}
where $P_{story,i}$ is the total gravity load carried by the storey, $H_i/\Delta_{H,i}$ the
first-order lateral stiffness obtained from the $W$ case, $h_i$ the storey height and
$R_M=0.85$. For columns, $B_1 = C_m/(1-P_r/P_{e1})\ge1$ with $C_m = 0.6-0.4M_1/M_2$
computed from the no-translation end moments and $P_{e1} = \pi^2\tau eEI/L^2$. Required
strengths are $M_r = B_1M_{nt} + B_2M_{lt}$ and $P_r = P_{nt}+B_2P_{lt}$; beams use the
larger $B_2$ of the two storeys they connect. A storey with $P_{story}\ge P_{e,story}$ is
unstable and is treated as failure.

The amplifiers are \emph{recomputed for every sample}: $P_{story}$, $P_r$ and $C_m$ follow the
sampled gravity loads, and $P_{e,story}$ and $P_{e1}$ follow the sampled modulus factor $e$. The
assumptions behind this use of the method are that (a) the lateral stiffness of a storey scales
with the frame-wide modulus factor and is otherwise that of the nominal sections, (b) the
gravity load is distributed over the columns as in the first-order analysis, (c) member stiffness
is not reduced by yielding before the first member limit state is reached, and (d) the storey
amplification is the same for all columns of a storey (rigid floor). Assumptions (a) and (b) are
removed in the full re-analysis of Section~\ref{sec:fullmc}, where every member has its own modulus
and property factor and the P-$\Delta$ (and, through the element geometric stiffness, P-$\delta$)
effects are computed directly; assumption (c) is inherent in the elastic ULS definition below. In
deterministic design, the Direct Analysis Method (DAM) \citep{AISC360,SurovekMaleck2004} is followed:
stiffness is reduced by $\tau = 0.8$, notional loads of $0.002Y_i$ are applied in gravity-only
combinations and an additional $0.001Y_i$ in all combinations so that $\tau_b=1$ may be used;
effective length factors are then $K=1$. In the reliability analysis the best-estimate stiffness
($\tau=1$) is used and the notional loads are replaced by the random out-of-plumbness $\theta$.

\subsection{Member resistances and limit states}\label{sec:resistance}

Nominal resistances follow AISC~360-16: compression from Chapter~E3 with $K=1$, strong-axis
buckling over the storey height and weak-axis buckling between floors (columns) or between
secondary beams at third points (beams); flexure from F2 (lateral--torsional buckling, $C_b=1$)
and F3 (flange local buckling); shear from G2.1; and combined forces from the H1-1 interaction
equations. Beam axial forces are small (largest beam $P_r/P_c$ of the optimised designs below
0.07), so the slender-web reduction of beam compression capacity is not modelled. For the
deterministic checks the LRFD resistance factors $\phi_c=\phi_b=\phi_t=0.9$ and $\phi_v=1.0$ or
$0.9$ (G2.1) are applied to the ASCE~7-05 \citep{ASCE705} strength combinations $1.4D$,
$1.2D+1.6L$, $1.2D+0.5L\pm1.6W$ and $0.9D\pm1.6W$ (roof live load is treated as part of $L$).
Serviceability requires inter-storey drift $\le h/400$ under $D+0.5L+W$ and beam live-load
deflection $\le$ span/360.

For each realisation the member utilisation $U_k$ is the larger of the H1-1 interaction ratio
and the shear ratio, both evaluated with nominal (unfactored) resistances that depend on the
random yield stress, modulus and fabrication factor of the member. Three limit-state functions
are defined:
\begin{align}
g_{ULS} &= 1 - \max_{s\in\{S_1,S_2\}}\max_k U_k^{(s)}, \label{eq:gULS}\\
g_{drift} &= 1 - \max_i \frac{\delta_i^{(S_2)}/h_i}{1/400}, \label{eq:gdrift}\\
g_{defl} &= 1 - \max_j \frac{\delta_{L,j}}{\ell_j/360}. \label{eq:gdefl}
\end{align}
The load scenarios are constructed with Turkstra's rule \citep{TurkstraMadsen1980}:
$S_1 = D + L_{50}$ (50-year maximum live load) and $S_2 = D + L_{apt} + W_{50}$
(arbitrary-point-in-time live load with the 50-year maximum wind); the out-of-plumbness acts in
both. The ULS event is the union of the member events over all members and both scenarios. It is
constructed sample by sample, so the dependence between members (common loads, common modulus,
partly correlated yield stress) and between the scenarios (common $D$, $E$, $F_y$, fabrication
factor and $\theta$) is represented exactly by the joint sampling; no bounds or independence
assumptions of series-system theory are needed. Turkstra's rule itself neglects the probability
that the 50-year live load and the 50-year wind coincide, which is small for the statistics used.
The drift SLS is evaluated in $S_2$ and the deflection SLS under $L_{50}$.

$g_{ULS}$ is a \emph{first-member-failure, second-order elastic, component-based} limit state:
the frame is deemed to fail when the first member reaches a code-format resistance, evaluated with
the AISC interaction equations and nominal (unfactored) resistances. It is not a collapse
analysis. It does not credit inelastic redistribution, and residual stresses and member
out-of-straightness enter only through the column-curve and LTB equations of AISC~360, not through
an explicit imperfect model. Connections are rigid and do not fail. Throughout this paper
$\beta_{ULS}$ denotes the reliability index of \emph{this} event. It is consistent with the
member-based calibration of current codes \citep{Ellingwood1980}, but it is a lower bound-type
measure of the collapse reliability, which inelastic system analyses report to be higher
\citep{Buonopane2006,Zhang2016}.

\subsection{Probabilistic model}\label{sec:probmodel}

Table~\ref{tab:rv} lists the basic random variables, their sources, and which parameters are
assumptions of this study. The load statistics are those of the probability-based load criterion
of \citet{Ellingwood1980} for dead load, 50-year live load and 50-year wind, together with the
arbitrary-point-in-time live load (gamma, mean $0.24L_n$, c.o.v.\ 0.60) and the fabrication
(geometric) factor (c.o.v.\ 0.05). These statistics were used to calibrate the LRFD load factors,
so using them makes the comparison between code-based and reliability-based designs internally
consistent. The c.o.v.\ of yield stress (0.07) and modulus (0.03) and the lognormal model are those
of the JCSS Probabilistic Model Code \citep{JCSS2001}. The mean-to-nominal ratio of $F_y$ (1.10)
and the inter-member equicorrelation of $\ln F_y$ ($\rho=0.5$) are assumptions; both are varied
in the sensitivity study. The initial out-of-plumbness is normal with zero mean and standard
deviation 1/1000, so that the erection tolerance of 1/500 corresponds to two standard deviations.

\emph{Dependence.} The load intensities are fully correlated over the frame, i.e.\ one realisation
of $D$, $L$ and $W$ scales the nominal load pattern. For dead load and wind this is the usual
assumption; for live load it neglects the spatial variability between floors and bays and is
conservative for the frame-level effects that govern here. The modulus factor is frame-wide in the
surrogate (members from one mill order) and member-specific in the full re-analysis; the yield
stress and fabrication factor are member-specific, the former with partial correlation to
represent a common heat or producer. $\theta$ is a single frame-wide sway imperfection.

\emph{Aleatory versus epistemic uncertainty.} The variables of Table~\ref{tab:rv} describe
physical (aleatory) variability, with their parameters fixed at the cited values. Model-form
(epistemic) uncertainty of the structural and resistance models, i.e.\ the error of the H1-1
interaction equations and of the second-order elastic analysis, is \emph{not} part of the
baseline model, so that the baseline indices are conditional on the structural model, as in the
code calibration that is the reference of the comparison. Its effect is quantified separately by
frame-wide lognormal model factors with unit mean on resistance ($\theta_R$), load effect
($\theta_E$) and drift ($\theta_\delta$), with c.o.v.\ of 0.05 and 0.10 chosen to bracket plausible
values (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Because H1-1 and the shear check are homogeneous of degree
one in the demands and of degree minus one in the resistances, the factors act on the
utilisation exactly as $U\theta_E/\theta_R$. Parameter uncertainty of the load statistics is
addressed by the sensitivity study. The model-factor analysis has two uses in this paper. First, it is a sensitivity study of the
final designs (Table~\ref{tab:modelunc}). Second, for the base frame and the three-storey frame, the
factors with c.o.v.\ 0.05 are included in the reliability constraints themselves (RBDO-C-MU,
Section~\ref{sec:optimisation}) to quantify the extra steel needed to keep the targets when this
uncertainty is counted. Because the baseline indices exclude model-form uncertainty, they
should not be read as estimates of real-world failure rates.

\emph{Dependence and spatial variability.} The loads are fully correlated over the frame (one
dead-load, one live-load and one wind factor per realisation), and the out-of-plumbness is a single
frame-wide variable. These assumptions suit the small regular frames studied here. They should not
be extrapolated to buildings in which the spatial variability of loads or imperfections is
substantial.

\subsection{Reliability estimation, confidence intervals and acceptance}\label{sec:mc}

Failure probabilities are estimated by crude Monte Carlo simulation. With $k$ failures in $N$
independent samples, the point estimate is $\hat P_f = k/N$, its c.o.v.\ is
$\sqrt{(1-P_f)/(NP_f)}$, and the reported index is $\hat\beta = -\Phi^{-1}(\hat P_f)$. Because $k$
is binomial, an exact (Clopper--Pearson) two-sided $95\%$ confidence interval
$[P_f^{lo},P_f^{hi}]$ is computed from the beta distribution,
\begin{equation}
P_f^{lo} = B^{-1}\!\left(0.025;\,k,\,N-k+1\right),\qquad
P_f^{hi} = B^{-1}\!\left(0.975;\,k+1,\,N-k\right),
\label{eq:cp}
\end{equation}
and mapped to the interval $[-\Phi^{-1}(P_f^{hi}),\,-\Phi^{-1}(P_f^{lo})]$ of $\beta$. The one-sided
$95\%$ lower confidence bound of $\beta$ follows from the one-sided upper bound
$B^{-1}(0.95;\,k+1,\,N-k)$ of $P_f$. When no failure is observed, the point estimate is undefined
and only the lower confidence bound is reported (for $N=10^6$, $P_f\le3.0\times10^{-6}$ and
$\beta\ge4.53$ with $95\%$ confidence). Every final design is reported with $N$, $k$, the point
estimate and the interval.

The limit states are evaluated by the superposition~\eqref{eq:superposition} and closed-form
resistances, so a sample costs a few vectorised arithmetic operations per member; a design is
evaluated with $5\times10^4$ samples in about one second on one CPU core, about four times the cost
of the five PyNite analyses.

\emph{Sample average approximation.} Inside the optimiser the samples are drawn once and reused
for every design (common random numbers). This SAA \citep{Kleywegt2002} makes $\hat\beta(\mathbf{x})$
a deterministic function of the design, so the comparison of two designs is not corrupted by
independent sampling noise (their estimates are positively correlated) and the discrete search
is well posed. The price is a \emph{finite-sample misclassification} risk: a design whose true
index is just below the target may be accepted because the particular sample is favourable, and
the optimiser, which seeks the lightest accepted design, preferentially finds such designs (the
optimisation bias of SAA). We control this risk in three ways: (i) every final design is
re-verified with $N=10^6$ \emph{independent} samples; (ii) the SAA problem is re-solved with other
sample streams and sizes ($N=2\times10^4$, $5\times10^4$, $10^5$) to measure the spread of the
optima; and (iii) a confidence-bounded variant imposes the targets on the one-sided $95\%$ lower
confidence bound of $\beta$ in the SAA sample instead of the point estimate. To save time, a
design is first evaluated on the first $10^4$ samples and the remainder is computed only if the
screening estimate is not clearly infeasible (more than 0.4 below a target index).

\emph{Three claims about a target.} For a target $\beta_T$ and an estimate from $N$ samples, three
statements of different strength are distinguished throughout the paper:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(P)] the \emph{point estimate} reaches the target, $\hat\beta\ge\beta_T$;
\item[(I)] the $95\%$ \emph{confidence interval contains} the target; the evidence is then inconclusive,
because the true index may lie on either side of $\beta_T$;
\item[(L)] the one-sided $95\%$ \emph{lower confidence bound} exceeds the target,
$\beta^{lcb}\ge\beta_T$. Only this statement establishes that the target is met with $95\%$ confidence.
\end{enumerate}
In the independent verification, each design and target is classified as ``above'' when the lower
end of the two-sided interval exceeds the target, so that (L) holds; ``within'' when the interval
contains the target, i.e.\ case (I); and ``below'' when the upper end is smaller than the target. A
``within'' result is \emph{not} reported as meeting the target. We say that such a design is
consistent with the target at the stated sampling resolution, and give the interval.

\emph{Two reliability constraints.} The optimiser imposes the targets in one of two ways. In the
point-estimate formulations (RBDO, RBDO-C) the SAA point estimates must reach the targets, which
corresponds to (P) on the SAA sample. In the confidence-bounded formulation (RBDO-C95,
Section~\ref{sec:optimisation}) the one-sided $95\%$ lower confidence bounds of the SAA estimates must
reach the targets, which corresponds to (L) on the SAA sample. The second formulation is the one
to use when the purpose is to demonstrate a prescribed minimum reliability with $95\%$ confidence. Its
designs are again verified with $10^6$ independent samples. Both formulations control only the Monte Carlo
\emph{sampling} uncertainty of the estimates. Neither removes systematic errors of the surrogate or of
the models (Sections~\ref{sec:fullmc} and~\ref{sec:strict}), and a lower confidence bound is conditional
on the model under which it is computed.

All reliability indices in this paper are conditional on the probabilistic model of
Table~\ref{tab:rv} and on the structural and resistance models. They do not include model-form
uncertainty unless this is stated explicitly (Sections~\ref{sec:probmodel} and~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Optimisation formulations}\label{sec:optimisation}

The objective is the steel mass $m(\mathbf{x}) = \rho_s\sum_k A_k(\mathbf{x})\ell_k$, used as a
proxy for material cost (connection, fabrication and erection costs are considered only in the
illustrative cost model of Section~\ref{sec:costmodel}). The formulations are
\begin{align}
\text{DDO-}h/n:\quad &\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ m\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\max_{c,k}U^{LRFD}_{c,k}\le1,\quad \frac{\delta}{h}\le\frac{1}{n},\quad \delta_L\le\frac{\ell}{360},\\
\text{RBDO:}\quad &\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ m\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\hat\beta_{ULS}\ge\beta_T,\quad \hat\beta_{drift}\ge\beta_{SLS},\quad \hat\beta_{defl}\ge\beta_{SLS},\\
\text{RBDO-C:}\quad &\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ m\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\text{the constraints of DDO-}h/400
\text{ and of RBDO},\\
\text{RBDO-C95:}\quad &\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ m\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\text{the constraints of DDO-}h/400,\nonumber\\
&\phantom{\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ m\quad\text{s.t.}\quad}\beta^{lcb}_{ULS}\ge\beta_T,\quad \beta^{lcb}_{drift},\,\beta^{lcb}_{defl}\ge\beta_{SLS},\\
\text{MO:}\quad &\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ \bigl(m,\,-\hat\beta_{ULS}\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\hat\beta_{drift},\hat\beta_{defl}\ge\beta_{SLS},\quad \hat\beta_{ULS}\ge1,
\end{align}
all subject to column areas that do not increase with height. DDO with $n=400$ (``DDO'') is the
code-based design; $n=450$, 500 and 600 represent the range of tighter drift limits used in
practice. RBDO-C is the formulation that a designer bound by the code could adopt: the
reliability constraints are added to, not substituted for, the mandatory checks, so that every
RBDO-C design is code-compliant and its mass is directly comparable with DDO. RBDO without the
code checks is retained to show what the reliability constraints alone would select; a design
accepted by RBDO but failing an LRFD check would have no regulatory standing under a
prescriptive code, although it could be admissible under a reliability-based (performance-based)
route of the kind allowed by EN~1990 \citep{EN1990}. RBDO-C95 replaces the SAA point estimates of RBDO-C by their
one-sided $95\%$ lower confidence bounds (Section~\ref{sec:mc}). It is the formulation to use when
the target is to be demonstrated with $95\%$ confidence. The point-estimate formulation RBDO-C is used
for the like-for-like comparisons with DDO, because DDO, too, is judged on nominal values. Two
further variants are used for the three-storey frame and the base frame: RBDO-C with an increased
ULS target that absorbs the surrogate discrepancy found in the validation (Section~\ref{sec:fullmc}),
and RBDO-C with the model factors of Section~\ref{sec:probmodel} included in the reliability
constraints (``RBDO-C-MU'').

The baseline targets are $\beta_T = 3.0$ for the ULS, the value adopted by \citet{Ellingwood1980}
for gravity load combinations in the calibration of the LRFD load factors (a lower value was
accepted for combinations with wind), and $\beta_{SLS}=1.5$ (50 years), the value given by EN~1990
\citep{EN1990} for irreversible serviceability states. Because both targets are choices of the
owner or code writer, results are also given for other targets.

DDO and RBDO(-C) are solved with the same elitist genetic algorithm (population 40, binary
tournament with Deb's feasibility rules \citep{Deb2000}, uniform crossover with probability 0.9,
per-gene mutation probability $1.5/n_v$ of which 70\% are steps of $\pm1$--3 catalogue positions
and 30\% random resets, and $(\mu+\lambda)$ replacement). A run stops after 15 generations without
improvement of the best design or after 60 generations. The best GA design is then polished by a
discrete local search that accepts the first feasible lighter neighbour among single down-sizing
moves and down/up-sizing swaps of two variables, repeated until no improvement is found, so every
reported optimum is locally optimal with respect to these moves. The GA is a heuristic: convergence
of repeated runs indicates robust convergence within the search budget, not a guarantee of global
optimality, which is checked only for the reduced problem by enumeration. The bi-objective
problem is solved with NSGA-II \citep{Deb2002} with constrained domination (population 48, 60
generations); all feasible designs visited are archived and the non-dominated archive is
re-verified. The SAA samples, the GA operators and the verification samples are all seeded, so
every number in this paper is reproducible from the released code.

\subsection{Illustrative cost model}\label{sec:costmodel}

To test whether conclusions drawn from steel mass carry over to cost, a simple cost index in
tonnes of steel equivalent is used,
\begin{equation}
C = m + \tau\, n_{sec} + \kappa\, m_{beam},
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $n_{sec}$ is the number of distinct section designations (procurement, detailing and
handling), $m_{beam}$ the beam mass (a proxy for the size of moment connections and their welding,
which scale with the beam flange force), and $\tau$ and $\kappa$ are cost ratios. The number of
members, connections and pieces is identical for all designs of a frame and therefore does not
change the comparison. Equation~\eqref{eq:cost} is not a calibrated cost model. The values of $\tau$ (0.25--1~t per
section designation) and $\kappa$ (0--0.25) are hypothetical sensitivity parameters, not estimates
of actual fabrication or connection costs, and the ranking obtained with them has not been checked
against project costs. The index is used only to show how large the non-mass terms would have to be
to change the ranking of the designs. All statements on economy in this paper therefore refer to
steel mass or to this illustrative index.
