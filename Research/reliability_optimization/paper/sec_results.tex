\section{Verification of the structural and reliability models}\label{sec:verification}

The conclusions of an optimisation study are only as credible as the models it optimises.
Four verification levels were therefore applied before any design comparison was made.

\subsection{Closed-form benchmarks}\label{sec:closedform}

Table~\ref{tab:closedform} compares PyNite with closed-form solutions for a cantilever, a
fixed--fixed beam under uniform load, and a fixed-base portal frame under lateral load. The
portal solution follows from the slope-deflection method for inextensible members, with
$k = (I_b/L)/(I_c/h)$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{Hh^3(2+3k)}{12EI_c(1+6k)},\qquad
M_{base} = \frac{Hh(1+3k)}{2(1+6k)},\qquad
M_{top} = \frac{3kHh}{2(1+6k)}.
\end{equation}
Agreement is at round-off level for the statically simple cases and within $10^{-5}$ for the
portal, the residual being the axial flexibility that PyNite includes. The second-order
response of a cantilever beam-column with axial load $P$ and tip load $H$ has the exact tip
sway $\Delta = \frac{H}{Pk}(\tan kL - kL)$, $k=\sqrt{P/EI}$. PyNite's P-$\Delta$ solver with
a single element is within 0.6\% of the exact value even at $P = 0.6P_{cr}$, and converges to
$<2\times10^{-6}$ with eight elements (Table~\ref{tab:closedform}), showing that its geometric
stiffness also captures the member (P-$\delta$) effect.

\subsection{Independent solver}\label{sec:opensees}

The optimised designs were rebuilt in OpenSeesPy \citep{McKenna2010,Zhu2018} with
elastic beam-column elements, element loads for the distributed gravity and self-weight
and nodal storey forces, independently of the PyNite model. Table~\ref{tab:opensees} lists the
largest relative difference of each response quantity, normalised by the largest absolute value
of that quantity in the frame. For all five unit cases the two solvers agree to
$10^{-13}$--$10^{-16}$, i.e.\ to machine precision. (Gravity-load drifts of these symmetric
frames are themselves of the order of round-off and are not compared.) For the P-$\Delta$
analysis of the combination $1.2D+0.5L+1.6W$, OpenSees used the \texttt{PDelta} coordinate
transformation and a load-controlled Newton solution. PyNite's second-order drifts differ from
OpenSees by at most 0.8\%, and the $B_1$--$B_2$ amplified first-order drifts used by the
surrogate by at most 1.8\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:pdelta}). The largest differences occur in the upper
storeys, where the drifts and hence the governing drift checks are small; in the lower storeys,
which govern the drift limit state, the surrogate exceeds the P-$\Delta$ solution by up to 1.8\%,
and it underestimates it by at most 1.0\% in any storey. Column base moments behave similarly (at
most 1.6\%). These differences are an order of magnitude smaller than the sampling resolution
of the reliability constraints at the SAA sample size (Section~\ref{sec:mcconv}).

\subsection{Reliability surrogate versus full second-order re-analysis}\label{sec:fullmc}

The surrogate rests on three simplifications: second-order effects through $B_1$--$B_2$, a
modulus that is uniform over the frame, and nominal section stiffness (the fabrication factor
enters the resistance only). In the full model, every sample is analysed with a PyNite
P-$\Delta$ analysis in which every member receives its own modulus factor (c.o.v.\ 0.03) and its
fabrication factor is applied to $A$ and $I$; the surrogate is evaluated on the same samples with
the mean of the member modulus factors. Because agreement of mean responses does not guarantee
agreement of small failure probabilities, the comparison was designed around the failure
classification and the tail, for eight final designs from six frame configurations (the base
DDO design and the RBDO-C designs of all seven frames, including nine storeys, 10-m bays and high
wind):
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{random set} of 600 samples of scenario $S_2$, which compares utilisation and drift and,
because $\beta_{drift}\approx1.5$ for these designs, the drift classification at the actual SLS
constraint level;
\item a \emph{tail-stratified set} drawn from a pool of $2\times10^5$ samples: stratum A contains
all samples the surrogate classifies as ULS failures ($U_s\ge1$; up to 250 analysed), and stratum B
the near misses $0.85\le U_s<1$ (250 analysed at random). Both scenarios are re-analysed when their
surrogate utilisation is in the tail. With the stratum probabilities known from the pool, the
full-model failure probability is estimated as $\hat P_f^{full} = P(A)\hat f_A + P(B)\hat f_B$,
where $\hat f$ is the fraction of full-model failures in a stratum. Samples with $U_s<0.85$ cannot
fail in the full model, since the largest observed ratio $U_{full}/U_s$ over all 8\,000 analysed
samples is 1.049. A $95\%$ interval of $\hat P_f^{full}$, of the ratio $\hat P_f^{full}/\hat P_f^{sur}$
and of the difference in $\beta$ is obtained by propagating Jeffreys posteriors of the stratum
fractions and of the pool counts by simulation.
\end{itemize}
The base RBDO-C design was in addition analysed under doubled wind (1000 samples) as a stress test
that produces failure fractions large enough to be compared directly.

Table~\ref{tab:validation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:validation} summarise the results. Over all designs
the surrogate overestimates the system utilisation by 0.4--1.1\% on average (standard deviation of
the difference 1.0--1.6\%) and is unbiased for drift ($-1.0$ to $+1.4\%$, standard deviation
1.9--3.1\%). In the random sets, 0 to 7 of 600 drift classifications differ (at most 1.2\%), and
the numbers of drift exceedances agree within their sampling scatter. In the tail, 79--91\% of the
surrogate failures are also failures of the full model and at most 4 of 250 near misses fail in
the full model. The resulting full-model failure probabilities differ from the surrogate ones by
$\Delta\beta_{ULS}$ between $-0.07$ and $+0.05$; for seven of the eight designs the $95\%$ interval of
$\Delta\beta$ contains zero. The exception is the three-storey frame, for which the full model gives
$\hat P_f^{full}=1.64\times10^{-3}$ against $1.31\times10^{-3}$ ($\Delta\beta=-0.07$, $95\%$
interval $[-0.17,-0.01]$): in this strength-governed, low-rise frame, four near misses fail in the
full model, so the surrogate is slightly unconservative. Under doubled wind (base design), the
failure fractions are 2.1\% (surrogate) and 1.7\% (full analysis), and the drift exceedance
fractions 68.1\% and 67.8\%.

In summary, the surrogate reproduces the full second-order reliability within about $\pm0.07$ in
$\beta_{ULS}$, which is of the order of the SAA resolution, and the drift reliability without
detectable bias. Where it errs, it errs on the conservative side in six of eight designs. The
three-storey result shows that the agreement is not guaranteed: a design accepted at the
target by the surrogate may have a full-model index up to about 0.1 lower, and a validation of this
kind should accompany any application of the surrogate to frame types outside the tested range.

\subsection{Monte Carlo convergence and sampling resolution}\label{sec:mcconv}

Fig.~\ref{fig:mcconv} shows the reliability indices of the base DDO and RBDO-C designs as
functions of the sample size for five independent random streams. At the SAA sample size
($N=5\times10^4$) the five streams give $\hat\beta_{ULS}$ between 2.97 and 3.16, i.e.\ a resolution
of about $\pm0.1$ with which the optimiser can enforce $\beta_T=3$; at $N=10^6$ they agree within
3.02--3.05 (c.o.v.\ of $\hat P_f$ about 3\%). The drift index converges much faster because its
failure probability is larger. The SAA estimate therefore guides the search, and the independent
$10^6$-sample estimate with its confidence interval is the one reported.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Optimiser robustness and global-search credibility}\label{sec:robust}

Table~\ref{tab:robust} reports repeated runs. For the base frame, ten DDO runs gave the best mass
of 18\,261~kg in eight runs and 18\,321~kg (+0.3\%) in two, and five RBDO-C runs with different
GA seeds all returned the same design (18\,787~kg). For the other frames, two independent
RBDO-C runs gave the same design in four of six frames; in the 6-m bay frame they differed by
0.1\% and in the high-wind frame a third run was made because the first two differed: the three
runs gave 21\,738, 21\,774 and 22\,078~kg (spread 1.6\%), the lightest coinciding with the design
found by RBDO without code constraints. The GA with local search is thus robust within the
tested budget for most frames, but not infallible; the reported optimum is always the lightest
design found over all runs, and it is a local optimum with respect to the moves of the local search.

For the reduced problem (one section group, three variables, 11\,200 designs), the exact optimum
was obtained by evaluating the designs in order of increasing mass until the first feasible one
(Table~\ref{tab:enum}). All ten GA runs of each formulation found the exact optimum, already
before the local search, using about 700 of the 11\,200 designs. The DDO optimum (21\,289~kg) is
preceded by 739 lighter, infeasible designs and the RBDO and RBDO-C optima (both 22\,383~kg,
+5.1\%) by 960. Grouping all storeys into one section group also shows the value of the design
grouping: the six-storey frame with three groups is 14\% (DDO) and 16\% (RBDO-C) lighter.

The SAA settings were varied for the base frame (Table~\ref{tab:saa}). With $N=2\times10^4$,
$5\times10^4$ (three independent sample streams) and $10^5$, RBDO-C returned the same design, whose
SAA estimates of $\beta_{ULS}$ ranged from 3.01 to 3.13 with the stream and size, while the
independent verification gives $3.049$ $[3.03, 3.07]$. Imposing the targets on the one-sided
$95\%$ lower confidence bounds in the SAA sample selected a heavier design (19\,079~kg, +1.6\%)
with $\hat\beta_{ULS}=3.125$ $[3.11, 3.14]$ and $\hat\beta_{drift}=1.551$; this is the price of
statistical conservatism at $N=5\times10^4$ for this frame.

A DDO run took 2--12~min and an RBDO-C run 10--56~min (median 32~min) on one CPU core, the
reliability evaluation accounting for a median of 83\% of the RBDO-C time.

\subsection{Deterministic versus code-compliant reliability-based design of the base frame}\label{sec:basedesign}

Table~\ref{tab:designs} compares the DDO and RBDO-C optima of the base frame. The DDO design
weighs 18\,261~kg; its governing LRFD ratio is 0.993 and its drift is 0.97 of the $h/400$ limit,
so both strength and drift are active. Verified with $10^6$ independent samples, it has
$k=1242$ ULS failures, $\hat\beta_{ULS}=3.025$ $[3.01, 3.04]$, statistically above the target,
but its 50-year drift reliability is only $\hat\beta_{drift} = 0.962$ ($\hat P_f = 0.17$): a drift
check at the nominal 50-year wind leaves about a one-in-six chance that the drift limit is
exceeded during the reference period. The reason is that the 50-year maximum wind has a
mean-to-nominal ratio of 0.78 but a c.o.v.\ of 0.37, so the nominal wind is exceeded with a
probability of about 0.19, and serviceability checks carry no load factor.

The RBDO-C design ($\beta_T = 3.0$, $\beta_{SLS}=1.5$) weighs 18\,787~kg (+2.88\%), satisfies all
LRFD checks (strength ratio 0.977) and has $\hat\beta_{ULS}=3.049$ $[3.03, 3.07]$ ($k=1148$) and
$\hat\beta_{drift}=1.507$ $[1.50, 1.51]$; both reliability constraints are active and both targets
are met with $95\%$ confidence. It is the same design as found by RBDO without code constraints,
so for the base frame the code checks are not binding once the reliability targets are imposed.
The additional steel is placed where it buys stiffness most cheaply: the lower-storey beams are
deepened (W21$\times$50 to W24$\times$55) and the interior columns of storeys 3--4 increased; its
deterministic drift ratio is 0.80 of $h/400$, i.e.\ $h/500$. The exceedance curves of
Fig.~\ref{fig:exceed} show that the two designs have virtually identical distributions of the
system utilisation, whereas the drift distribution of the RBDO-C design is shifted to the left
over its whole range.

\subsection{Cost--reliability trade-off}\label{sec:pareto}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the non-dominated designs of the bi-objective problem (mass versus
$\beta_{ULS}$, subject to $\beta_{drift},\beta_{defl}\ge1.5$), re-verified with $4\times10^5$
independent samples and shown with their $95\%$ intervals, together with all feasible designs
visited by NSGA-II (SAA estimates). The lightest front design (18\,747~kg) fails the LRFD strength
check (ratio 1.10) and has $\hat\beta_{ULS}=2.36$; all other front designs are code-compliant.
Because drift governs the mass once $\beta_{SLS}=1.5$ is imposed, the code-compliant front is
steep: raising $\hat\beta_{ULS}$ from 3.06 to 3.36, 3.64 and 3.83 costs +1.3\%, +3.5\% and +4.2\% of
steel (19\,039, 19\,452 and 19\,568~kg against 18\,787~kg). All front designs have $\hat\beta_{drift}$
between 1.51 and 1.57. The verified indices of the high-reliability front designs are lower than
their SAA estimates (3.83 against 4.26 for the heaviest), which illustrates the SAA optimisation
bias at small failure counts: the SAA sample contains only a few failures there, and the
optimiser selects designs for which the particular sample is favourable. The front passes through
the RBDO-C optimum, which is an independent check of both optimisers. The DDO optimum lies to the
left of the front but is infeasible for it because $\hat\beta_{drift}=0.96$.

\subsection{Effect of the reliability targets and of the deterministic drift limit}\label{sec:targets}

Table~\ref{tab:targets} isolates the role of the targets for the base frame. With $\beta_T=3.0$ and
$\beta_{SLS}=0.5$, RBDO returns \emph{exactly} the DDO design: the code-based optimum is also the
reliability-based optimum when the reliability levels it implicitly provides are required. Raising
$\beta_{SLS}$ to 1.0 and 1.5 costs +0.97\% and +2.88\% of steel, and raising $\beta_T$ to 3.5 at
$\beta_{SLS}=1.5$ costs +6.52\% ($\hat\beta_{ULS}=3.67$, above the target because of the
discreteness of the catalogue).

Table~\ref{tab:driftlimits} and Fig.~\ref{fig:driftlimits} compare RBDO-C with deterministic
optima for drift limits of $h/400$, $h/450$, $h/500$ and $h/600$, all with the same strength and
deflection checks. Tightening the drift limit raises $\beta_{drift}$ of the drift-governed frames
in a similar way (about 1.2--1.4 at $h/450$, 1.5 at $h/500$ and 2.0 at $h/600$), but it has no
effect on frames governed by strength (three storeys, low wind) and cannot raise their
$\beta_{ULS}$. Applying the acceptance rule of Section~\ref{sec:mc} to the verified indices, the
lightest deterministic design that meets both targets is $h/500$ for the base, nine-storey, 6-m bay
and high-wind frames and $h/600$ for the 10-m bay and low-wind frames; for the three-storey frame no
drift limit meets $\beta_T$. Compared with that design, RBDO-C is equal (base), lighter by 0.10\%
(nine storeys), 0.34\% (low wind), 1.14\% (high wind) and 4.99\% (10-m bays), and heavier by
0.58\% for the 6-m bays. The last case is instructive: the $h/500$ design has
$\hat\beta_{drift}=1.499$ $[1.495, 1.503]$, statistically indistinguishable from the target and
therefore accepted in verification, but its SAA estimate (1.494) was below the target, so the
point-estimate SAA constraint rejected it. Near-threshold classification can thus change the
optimum by about 0.5\% of mass in either direction. No single drift limit delivers the target
reliabilities in all frames, which is what the explicit reliability constraints achieve by
construction.

\subsection{Parametric study and statistical verification of all final designs}\label{sec:parametric}

Table~\ref{tab:verification} reports, for every frame, the DDO, RBDO and RBDO-C optima with their
masses, LRFD ratios, failure counts and $95\%$ intervals, and Fig.~\ref{fig:parametric} shows the
same results graphically. Four observations stand out.

First, the reliability of deterministic optima is far from uniform. Although every DDO design has
a governing strength or drift check within 7\% of its limit, the verified system indices range
from $\hat\beta_{ULS}=2.880$ $[2.87, 2.89]$ (10-m bays) to 3.656 $[3.61, 3.70]$ (6-m bays), and the
drift indices from 0.887 (high wind) to 2.544 (three storeys). Three DDO designs fall
significantly below $\beta_T=3.0$ (three storeys, 10-m bays, low wind) and five have
$\hat\beta_{drift}<1.5$, four of them below 1.0.

Second, RBDO-C equalises the reliability. All seven RBDO-C designs satisfy every LRFD check and
meet both targets under the acceptance rule: six are above both targets with $95\%$ confidence, and
the three-storey design has $\hat\beta_{ULS}=2.995$ $[2.98, 3.01]$, within the interval of the target.
The mass premium relative to DDO, computed from unrounded masses, ranges from +1.13\% (low wind)
to +10.11\% (high wind). The premium is essentially the cost of raising $\beta_{drift}$ to 1.5 in
the drift-governed frames and of raising $\beta_{ULS}$ to 3.0 in the strength-governed ones.

Third, the code checks matter in two of seven frames. Without them, RBDO selects a 6-m bay design
with an LRFD strength ratio of 1.011 and a low-wind design with 1.002, i.e.\ designs that meet the
reliability targets but would marginally fail the mandatory strength check; in the 6-m bay frame
RBDO trades the surplus ULS reliability of the DDO design ($\hat\beta_{ULS}=3.66$) for stiffness
($\hat\beta_{ULS}=3.22$). Adding the code checks (RBDO-C) raises the mass by 0.32\% (6-m bays) and
0.64\% (low wind) relative to RBDO. In the other five frames the RBDO optimum already satisfies the
code checks and RBDO-C returns the same design. The conclusions on mass are therefore unchanged
when all RBDO candidates must satisfy the deterministic LRFD checks; the premiums over DDO change
from +6.67\% to +7.01\% (6-m bays) and from +0.48\% to +1.13\% (low wind).

Fourth, the location of the extra steel explains the premiums (Tables~\ref{tab:members} and
\ref{tab:cost}). In the drift-governed frames (base, nine storeys, high wind) RBDO-C adds steel to
the beams of the lower storey groups and to the interior columns, which stiffens the frame; in the
high-wind frame this adds 755~kg to columns and 1\,240~kg to beams. In the 10-m bay frame, where
DDO falls below $\beta_T$ because the long beams are governed by the 50-year live load (95\% of
failures in $S_1$, 95\% in beams), RBDO-C deepens the beams (+577~kg) and lightens the columns
($-151$~kg), after which the remaining failures are shared between beams (57\%) and columns
(43\%). In the 6-m bay frame, governed by drift but with short, lightly loaded beams, failures occur
mostly in interior columns (77--79\%) in the wind scenario.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the probabilistic model and to model uncertainty}\label{sec:sensitivity}

Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} shows how the verified reliability indices of the base designs change
when one assumption of Table~\ref{tab:rv} is altered ($4\times10^5$ samples each). Among the
physical variables, the system ULS index is most sensitive to the live-load model (c.o.v.\ 0.35
instead of 0.25: $\Delta\beta\approx-0.5$), the fabrication factor (removing it: $+0.3$) and the
yield-stress model (mean-to-nominal 1.05 instead of 1.10, or c.o.v.\ 0.10 instead of 0.07: $-0.2$).
Inter-member correlation of $F_y$ changes $\beta_{ULS}$ by $\pm0.05$, and the out-of-plumbness and
modulus variability are negligible. The drift index depends almost exclusively on the wind model:
a mean-to-nominal ratio of 0.90 instead of 0.78 lowers it by 0.4, and the c.o.v.\ changes it by
about $\pm0.2$. The DDO and RBDO-C designs react in the same way, so the comparison between them is
robust to these assumptions.

Model uncertainty has a larger effect on the absolute ULS indices (Table~\ref{tab:modelunc}).
Frame-wide model factors on resistance, load effect and drift with c.o.v.\ 0.05 lower $\beta_{ULS}$ by
0.23--0.30 and with c.o.v.\ 0.10 by 0.82--0.90 in all frames, for DDO and RBDO-C alike, whereas
$\beta_{drift}$ changes by at most 0.05 because the drift uncertainty is dominated by the wind. The
baseline indices of this paper are therefore conditional on the structural and resistance models:
with a model uncertainty of 0.10, a design meeting $\beta_T=3.0$ in the baseline model would have
an index of about 2.1--2.3. Because the shift is almost uniform across frames and formulations,
the \emph{relative} comparison of DDO and RBDO-C is not affected; a target that is to be met
including model uncertainty would have to be raised by the corresponding amount, at the mass cost
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Answer to the research question}
For the frames studied, uncertainty-aware optimisation did \emph{not} produce lighter designs than
deterministic optimisation against the LRFD checks; the code-compliant reliability-based designs
were 1.1--10.1\% heavier. The deterministic optima are already close to the reliability-based
optimum for the ULS ($\hat\beta_{ULS}\approx2.9$--3.7 at a target of 3.0), while the conventional
drift check at the nominal 50-year wind provides only $\hat\beta_{drift}\approx0.9$--1.2 for the
drift-governed frames. When the reliability levels implicit in the code are required, RBDO returns
the DDO design exactly (Table~\ref{tab:targets}). RBDO therefore provides economy \emph{at a
prescribed and uniform reliability}, not with respect to code-based design, whose reliability is
unknown and non-uniform. Against deterministic designs with a tightened drift limit that meet the
same targets, RBDO-C was lighter by up to 5.0\% in five frames, equal in one and 0.6\% heavier in
one, the last because of near-threshold sampling classification. These conclusions agree in sign
with the observation of \citet{Papadrakakis2004} that a reliability-based steel-frame design was
heavier than its deterministic counterpart.

\paragraph{What the reliability indices mean}
$\beta_{ULS}$ is the index of the first attainment of a code-format member limit state in a
second-order elastic analysis (Section~\ref{sec:resistance}). It is consistent with the
component-based calibration of the LRFD factors, which is what makes the comparison with the code
meaningful, but it is neither a collapse reliability nor an absolute measure of safety. Three
omissions act in opposite directions. Inelastic redistribution in these redundant frames would
raise the collapse reliability \citep{Buonopane2006,Zhang2016}. Residual stresses and member
imperfections are represented only through the AISC column and LTB curves, not through an explicit
imperfect model, and connection behaviour is not modelled. Model uncertainty, if included, lowers
the indices by up to about 0.9 for a c.o.v.\ of 0.10 (Table~\ref{tab:modelunc}). The validation
against full P-$\Delta$ re-analysis (Section~\ref{sec:fullmc}) removes the surrogate approximations
but not these model-form choices. The reported indices should therefore be read as conditional,
comparative measures, appropriate for comparing designs and formulations under one consistent
model, and not as estimates of real-world failure rates.

\paragraph{Statistical confidence and acceptance}
With $N=10^6$ independent samples and 400--2000 ULS failures per final design, the $95\%$ intervals
of $\beta_{ULS}$ are about $\pm0.02$--$0.05$, and of $\beta_{drift}$ about $\pm0.005$. The reliability
constraints are imposed on SAA point estimates with $N=5\times10^4$, whose resolution is about
$\pm0.1$ in $\beta_{ULS}$; designs found by the optimiser therefore lie up to about 0.1 on either
side of the target in verification, and three of 21 final designs (Table~\ref{tab:verification})
are statistically indistinguishable from the target. Requiring the SAA lower confidence bound to
meet the target removes this ambiguity at a cost of 1.6\% of steel for the base frame
(Table~\ref{tab:saa}). Which rule is appropriate depends on whether the target is understood as a
point value (as in code calibration) or as a value to be demonstrated with confidence.

\paragraph{Cost of reliability}
The trade-off curves are strongly asymmetric. Once a frame is drift-governed, its ULS reliability
can be raised from 3.0 to about 3.8 for 4\% more steel (Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}), whereas the
serviceability index is expensive to raise (2.9\% of steel from $\beta_{drift}=0.96$ to 1.51 in the
base frame and 10.1\% from 0.89 to 1.51 in the high-wind frame). Because the ULS and SLS indices
of a design are governed by different scenarios and random variables (Table~\ref{tab:members},
Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}), a single global safety measure would hide these trade-offs.

\paragraph{What has and has not been demonstrated}
Demonstrated: for regular planar steel moment frames of three to nine storeys with 6--10-m bays under
gravity and wind, with discrete W-shapes and the stated probabilistic model, (i) the superposition
surrogate reproduces full second-order Monte Carlo results within about $\pm0.07$ in $\beta_{ULS}$; (ii)
the GA with local search finds the exact optimum of a reduced problem in all runs and repeats its
full-problem results in most frames; and (iii) code-compliant RBDO costs 1--10\% more steel than DDO
and is generally the least-mass way to meet both targets. Not demonstrated: three-dimensional
frames and torsion, multiple wind directions, seismic actions, inelastic collapse reliability,
connection and foundation design, cost models calibrated to fabrication practice, and live-load
models with spatial variability. A three-objective NSGA-II run (mass, $\beta_{ULS}$, $\beta_{drift}$)
with about 3500 designs did not approach the single-objective optima (lightest design with both
targets 19\,500~kg against 18\,787~kg), so for discrete frame problems of this size an
$\varepsilon$-constraint approach with a strong single-objective optimiser, complemented by a
bi-objective front, is the more economical way to map the trade-off.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A reproducible framework for reliability-constrained optimisation of steel moment frames was
developed on the open-source PyNite library and applied to seven planar frames with discrete
W-shape sections. The main conclusions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Five linear analyses per design, combined by superposition with AISC $B_1$--$B_2$
amplification, give a reliability surrogate that agrees with OpenSeesPy to machine precision for
linear responses and within 2\% for P-$\Delta$ responses. Against full P-$\Delta$ re-analysis with
member-by-member random stiffness, tail-stratified for eight designs, it reproduces $\beta_{ULS}$
within $-0.07$ to $+0.05$ and the drift reliability without detectable bias.
\item Deterministic minimum-mass designs that satisfy the LRFD checks have non-uniform reliability:
$\hat\beta_{ULS}$ between 2.88 and 3.66, three of seven significantly below 3.0, and 50-year
$\hat\beta_{drift}$ between 0.89 and 2.54.
\item Code-compliant RBDO with $\beta_T=3.0$ and $\beta_{SLS}=1.5$ met both targets in independent
$10^6$-sample verification (six designs with $95\%$ confidence, one within the interval of the
target) and was 1.1--10.1\% heavier than DDO. Without the code checks, RBDO selected two designs
that marginally fail the LRFD strength check; enforcing the checks added 0.3--0.6\% of steel and did
not change any conclusion. With code-implied targets, RBDO reproduces the DDO design.
\item Uncertainty-aware optimisation therefore did not reduce steel mass relative to code-based
optimisation; it delivered designs with prescribed, uniform reliability, generally at less steel
than tightened deterministic drift limits that meet the same targets. These conclusions hold under
an illustrative cost model with section-count and connection terms.
\item The absolute indices are conditional on the structural model and the load statistics: model
uncertainty with c.o.v.\ 0.10 lowers $\beta_{ULS}$ by about 0.9, and the live- and wind-load models
change the indices by up to 0.5; the relative comparison of the formulations is insensitive to these
choices.
\end{enumerate}
